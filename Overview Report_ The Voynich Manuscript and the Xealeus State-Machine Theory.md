1. Introduction: The Voynich Enigma & Historical Context  
The Voynich Manuscript (Yale University, Beinecke Rare Book and Manuscript Library, MS 408) stands as one of the most resilient cryptological and computational enigmas in information history. Carbon-dated via accelerator mass spectrometry to the early 15th century (1404–1438), this 240-page vellum codex contains an unmapped script utilizing a character set of 20 to 30 distinct glyphs. The text is framed by complex, hand-drawn medieval illustrations categorized into botanical folios, astrological and cosmological diagrams, anatomical or balneological scenes featuring nude women in plumbing networks, and pharmaceutical recipes.  
For over a century, quantitative attempts to decode the manuscript under standard cryptographic or natural language frameworks have stalled. The primary impediment is a set of mutually contradictory statistical characteristics:  
- Global Rank-Frequency Compliance: At the macro-textual level, the word frequencies strictly follow Zipf’s Law, exhibiting a log-log linear distribution identical to human languages wherein the frequency of any word token is inversely proportional to its rank.  
- Abnormal Binomial Word-Length Distribution: At the lexical level, the text fundamentally violates vocalic and consonantal graphotactics found in natural human prose. Word lengths do not exhibit the characteristically skewed, long-tailed asymmetric distribution generated by short function words in natural languages. Instead, Voynich tokens follow a tight, symmetric binomial distribution centered precisely around an arithmetical mean of 5.5 characters, with an extreme underrepresentation of both single-character tokens and long words (>9 characters).  
Key Takeaway: The core historical enigma of the Voynich Manuscript lies in its information-theoretic contradiction: macro-structurally, it mimics semantic prose encoding by conforming to Zipf’s Law, yet micro-structurally, it violates natural linguistic morphology via a strict binomial word-length distribution centered at 5.5 characters and a pervasive absence of phrase-level syntax.  
Because natural languages depend on variable-length functional morphemes, traditional cryptanalysis vacillated between classifying the document as an unknown natural language dialect, a complex polyalphabetic cipher, or an elaborate medieval forgery. However, evaluating the manuscript through modern computational linguistics reveals that these historical frameworks fail because they erroneously assume a semantic prose generative model.  
Moving past these historical dead-ends requires a fundamental paradigm shift away from semantic decipherment and toward computational state-machine reconstruction.

1. Elimination of Interlinear Data Leakage: The raw IVTFF archive embeds 3 to 6 parallel transcriptions per physical line (such as ;H> Takahashi, ;C> Currier, ;F> Friedman, and ;U> Stolfi). Failing to isolate a single transcription line causes duplicated copies of identical physical lines to straddle machine-learning training and validation splits. Strictly filtering for the ;H> (Takahashi) transcription isolates a single authoritative reading and completely eliminates cross-validation data leakage.  
2. True Word-Level Tokenization: Default string parsers frequently misinterpret period-delimited strings as monolithic tokens. Splitting strictly on physical word-break periods (.) and commas (,) while removing non-textual alignment fillers (!, %) isolates 34,674 true Voynich tokens across Currier A and Currier B.
